# Latent safety filters still trust the encoder

The claim is half open. Its repair step is taken. Cao & Bansal (arXiv 2609.34300, 28 Sep 2026) already take the minimum of a learned latent Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) safety value over a calibrated set that adapts online. An HJ safety value is a number whose sign says whether failure can still be avoided from a state; "latent" means it is computed on the compressed state a world model learns from observations. Older grid-based work does the same with physical state-estimate sets and already reports the cost in conservatism. Its diagnosis step is not taken: no paper we found measures how a learned or latent safety value fails under perception error as a function of distance to the failure boundary. The source of the set is also open. Every calibrated latent filter bounds the world model's *prediction* error and treats the encoder's reading of the current observation as truth. None builds the set from the tracker's own uncertainty, and none grows a set while the object is lost or the measurement is late. No paper does any of this for a free payload on a carried tray, bimanual or not. The publishable claim is therefore narrower than the one stated: tracker error breaks latent values near the boundary, worst during track loss, and a set built from the tracker fixes this where a set built from the world model's residual cannot. Two warnings apply throughout. Each "nobody has done X" rests on what four searches did not find, not on proof. And Cao & Bansal were read at abstract level only, one week after release, so read their full paper before claiming any difference.

## The repair is taken; the diagnosis and the set's source are open

**Verdict.** Five parts of the claim are already taken. (1) Taking the minimum of a learned latent HJ value over a calibrated set: Cao & Bansal build adaptive conformal inference (ACI) sets, which widen or shrink after each observed error, from the gap between the latent the world model predicted and the latent the encoder infers from the next observation, and they test on simulation and hardware ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34300)). (2) Turning a perception-error set into a robust HJ or barrier margin and measuring how much safe set it costs: RoVer-CoRe sweeps error coverage from 0 to 1 on a grid HJ value ([Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14755)), GUARDIAN takes the minimum of a grid value over a certified estimate set ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06026)), and Jahanshahi & Chen tighten a barrier-value function with a conformal bound on estimation error ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13819)). (3) Calibrated uncertainty inside latent reachability on manipulation hardware: UNISafe ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00779)). (4) Evidence that perception errors cause failures that depend on the state: the Bansal group's reachability analysis of vision controllers ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21736)). (5) Sets that grow during occlusion or latency, for hand-built filters: OcclusionCBF and Thumm et al. ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06342); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15221)). Three parts are open. Nobody has measured a learned or latent value's **false-safe rate** (the share of states it calls safe that go on to fail) against distance to the boundary under tracker noise, delay and dropout; the Bansal work measures controllers, not values. Nobody builds a latent value's set from the tracker; Kim et al. name this the **estimation gap** but close it with extra training signals, not with a worst case ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06492)). And nobody does perception-robust safety for a free payload on a carried tray. The claim is open only if it is stated as an estimation-gap claim, measured at the boundary, under track loss and latency.

### Cao & Bansal fix a world model that imagines wrong; we target a tracker that sees wrong

Cao & Bansal's set comes from the residual between the world model's predicted latent and the "observation-inferred" latent ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34300)). That residual treats the encoder's output as ground truth. It catches a model that predicts badly. It cannot catch an observation that is itself wrong, because a wrong observation yields a wrong "truth" and the residual looks normal. It also needs a fresh observation to compute. During a dropout there is none. Worse, a Kalman filter coasting on its own prediction feeds the encoder a ball state that agrees with a smooth prediction, so the residual reads near zero exactly while the ball is hidden near the rim. ACI then widens its set only after the error shows up, and that lag eats lead time. Our set comes from the tracker: the sphere-fit and Kalman covariance, calibrated by conformal prediction (CP, a method that turns held-out errors into a set containing the truth with a chosen probability), and grown with the time since the last good fit. That set grows forward during the loss, before any error is seen. These points are our inferences from their abstract and our design, not results; Experiment 2 tests them. Where we do **not** differ: the operation itself, the minimum of a learned HJ value over a calibrated set, is theirs, and we must cite it as such. The two sets also combine naturally: theirs covers the prediction gap, ours the estimation gap.

### UNISafe flags the unfamiliar; a mismeasured ball looks familiar

UNISafe measures epistemic uncertainty, meaning how unfamiliar an input is to the world model, calibrates a threshold on it with CP, and runs reachability in a latent-plus-uncertainty space on a Franka ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00779)). A noisy point cloud of a ball in a well-covered state is familiar. Its epistemic score stays low while its latent is wrong. Cao & Bansal's own abstract argues that ensemble disagreement and value-consistency residuals "can remain small" even when predictions deviate ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34300)). UNISafe guards against the unknown; our claim guards against the known but mismeasured. Kim et al. are the only latent-filter paper that separates the estimation gap (the current latent lacks the safety-relevant state) from the prediction gap (failures show only after they happen). They fix it with privileged thermal or tactile training signals and a single conformal threshold, on a Franka FR3; their opaque-bottle rice-pouring task is the closest published analogue to a hidden payload hazard ([Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06492)).

### Where the difference is thin

If the shipped value takes the tracked ball state directly, as a low-dimensional physical latent, then "minimum of the value over the Kalman ellipsoid" is the measurement-robust barrier construction of Dean and Cosner, or GUARDIAN's, applied to a learned value ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16001); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06026)). Reviewers will say so. Two defences survive. The boundary-resolved measurement and the growth of the set during track loss are new either way. And a genuinely learned encoder, point cloud to latent, puts the estimation gap in latent space, where nobody has bounded it. Without one of these, the work is an application paper.

### (a) Learned and latent filters calibrate the model, not the sensor

### (b) Perception-error sets reach hand-built and grid values, never learned ones

The step from error bound to safety margin is now routine. It takes four forms: a margin in a **measurement-robust control barrier function** (MR-CBF, a barrier whose margin covers state-estimate error), a conformal quantile, a distributionally robust tightening, or a tube in robust MPC. The research frontier is getting a tight bound: state-dependent, adaptive or learned. In every certified case the value or barrier is hand-built, analytic or computed on a grid. Point clouds appear only in Yang et al. (LiDAR) and Long et al. (xArm6). **Zonotopes** (a centre plus a sum of line segments, cheap to add and map) and ellipsoids wrap a black-box perception module in a low-dimensional state; none passes through a learned world model.

### (c) Track loss and latency are handled as set growth, never in a learned filter

Track loss and latency are one mathematical object: a set that grows with the age of the last good measurement, at roughly maximum speed times age. Driving and human-robot work use this recipe with hand-built models. No learned or latent filter does. No paper derives a required perception latency for a latent filter or for any manipulation task. Failure-prediction papers report lead time but never subtract perception latency from it.

## Pitch the estimation gap, then run three experiments

Pitch one sharp claim, not a framework. A suggested form: *latent safety filters calibrate what the world model predicts and trust what the tracker reports. On a tray with a free rolling ball, tracker error breaks the latent value near the failure boundary, worst during track loss. A set built from the tracker, calibrated by conformal prediction and grown with the age of the last good fit, restores safety; a set built from the world-model residual does not, because during a loss the residual has nothing to measure.* Present it as the complement to Cao & Bansal (prediction gap) and to Kim et al. (estimation gap closed by more information): we close the estimation gap with robustness. Cite RoVer-CoRe's stated future work, reformulating HJ values for partial observability, as the opening ([Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14755)).

Keep VLM grounding out of the headline. Its column is empty, but grounding errors are discrete (the wrong segment) rather than metric, and adding them turns a sharp claim into a framework. Treat it as an extension; unions of ellipsoids (GM-IPC) are a ready tool for multi-modal grounding error ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04329)).

Place the robust margin where the data is freshest. Beger et al. prove that a filter with fresh measurements tolerates more disturbance than a delayed predictive one, and their best design layers both ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29792)). For us, that means tightening the FL-MPC's QP rows (tilt, acceleration) with the tracker set at control rate, while the task-space latent monitor runs at camera rate. CSymPlan and CORD-SLS are the nearest models for tightening a feedback-linearized or robust MPC with perception error ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22983); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14188)).

Two caveats belong in the paper. Conformal sets assume test errors look like calibration errors, and a filter's own interventions shift the state distribution; a prior project note records that Mirzaeedodangeh et al. show this breaks conformal barrier bounds and fix it with iterative policy updates ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15366)). So calibrate under the filtered policy, not the nominal one. And a ray-cast depth sensor with a chosen noise model invites the charge that we picked the noise. Fit the noise to real depth images of the real ball before any sweep.

### Experiment 1: map the false-safe rate against distance to the boundary

This experiment proves the first half of the claim, and nobody has run it for a learned value. Use privileged simulation to get the true value or the true time until the ball leaves the tray. Bin states by that distance to the boundary. Perturb the tracker three ways: scale the sphere-fit noise; add delay (for example 0, 33, 66, 100 and 200 ms); and drop the ball for scripted windows, including occlusion by the arms or tray rim near the edge. In each bin, report the false-safe rate of the latent value and of a privileged-state value; the gap between them isolates perception error from value-approximation error. The Bansal group showed state-dependent failure for controllers ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21736)) and GUARDIAN ran a vulnerability analysis on a grid ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06026)); this would be the first such curve for a learned or latent value. Pre-register the kill: if the false-safe rate near the boundary is not clearly above the far-field rate (say three times), the "moments that matter" claim fails. If FL-MPC's existing margins already absorb the error, the claim is moot for this system, and that is worth knowing before writing.

### Experiment 2: race tracker sets against residual sets at equal conservatism

This experiment proves the second half and is the one that decides the paper. Run five shields over the same value: no margin; one global conformal threshold (Kim-style); an epistemic threshold (UNISafe-style); ACI over the predicted-versus-observed latent residual with minimum-of-value (Cao & Bansal-style); and ours, the minimum of the value over a CP-calibrated tracker set. Make our set state-dependent, binned by visible fraction of ball points and distance to the rim, since a one-sided view biases a sphere fit; state-dependent CP is the established cure for the conservatism of a single global quantile ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.21308)). Grow it by maximum ball speed times age during dropout and latency. Report trade-off curves of roll-off or spill rate against intervention rate and task time, so that every shield is compared at the same conservatism. Split results by phase: steady tracking, dropout, and re-acquisition. Our hypothesis is that ACI matches us during steady tracking and loses during dropout near the rim. Pre-register the kill: if ACI matches the tracker set within confidence intervals during dropout windows at equal intervention rate, the set's source does not matter and the novelty claim should be dropped.

### Experiment 3: derive a latency budget from the lead time, then test it

Every latency result in the literature has the same shape: needed clearance is about closing speed × latency + speed²/(2 × braking authority) ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01855)). For the tray, define the lead time at each state as the time until the ball reaches the rim, minus the time FL-MPC needs to level or counter-tilt the tray under torque limits. The requirement is sensing + sphere fit + Kalman update + value + actuation time below that margin. As a sense of scale, a ball at 0.3 m/s moves 3 cm in 100 ms; at 3 cm from the rim, 100 ms of delay spends its whole lead time. Sweep injected latency and check that the false-safe "knee" falls where the formula predicts, as the RADAR paper does for its speed bound. Then build a per-state tolerable-delay map, the continuous analogue of the delay shield's controllability value ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02164)). Two figures from project memory, not re-checked here, make this binding: latent-HJ evaluation took about 190 ms, and the real Piper's feed-forward torque cap (about ±8 Nm) is about 12 times tighter than the simulator's 100 Nm, so real recovery is slower than simulated recovery. Use the real cap when computing lead time.

## Conclusion

The field has settled on "calibrate the dynamics, trust the encoder", and the signals it calibrates are blind in a specific way. Epistemic scores stay low for a familiar but mismeasured input. Prediction residuals need a fresh observation and read near zero when a coasting tracker agrees with the model. Both failures concentrate where the ball is hidden near the rim, which is exactly where lead time is shortest. The contribution is therefore not a new margin operator. It is a demonstration that the *source* of the set decides safety at the boundary, with Experiment 2 as the test that can kill it.

Track loss, latency and tracker noise are one object here: a set that grows with the age of the last good measurement. Treating them that way turns three columns of the gap table into one mechanism, and it yields the latency budget as a by-product rather than a separate study. That single mechanism, tested on hardware with a real depth sensor, is the paper. The VLM layer and the bimanual framing are context that make the setting new, not claims that need their own proof.
